## 1. Principles

Environmental storytelling = arranging a careful selection of objects and events so they suggest a story to the player who sees them. In 2D platformers it's the cheapest storytelling you have (zero momentum cost) and often the strongest.

- **Discovery beats delivery.** Limbo and Inside let the player discover the story instead of feeding it through cutscenes; their worlds never hand over a tidy beginning, middle and end.
- **Reward what the player already wants to do.** Hollow Knight works because it knows players want to explore and fight, so it hands out fragments of story as rewards for that exploration rather than dumping narrative on them.
- **Concrete, then ambiguous.** Every individual scene should be legible (you can say what you see). The *meaning* can stay open. Ambiguity built on confusion just feels unfinished.
- **One inference per room.** Each room should let the player infer at most one new thing. Stack too many and they'll infer none.

## 2. The layer stack

Design each important room in four layers:

| Layer | Content | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Foreground (play)** | what the player does | a crumbling bridge you must cross fast |
| **Midground (props)** | objects on the path | abandoned tools, a child's drawing, a barricade built from the inside |
| **Background (events)** | things happening beyond reach | a line of workers walking in step; a figure watching; a distant city burning |
| **Atmosphere** | light, color, weather, sound | light turns colder as you go deeper; a machine hum that grows |

Inside tells much of its story in the background layer and through the player's own body.

## 3. Techniques catalog

- **Before/after pairs:** show a place whole in one chapter and broken (or rebuilt) later. The change is the story.
- **Barricades tell direction:** a barricade built on the inside means people were keeping something *out*; built on the outside, keeping something *in*.
- **Wear patterns:** a worn path to one door; scratch marks at child height; a chair turned toward a window.
- **Object in the wrong place:** a toy in a factory, a flower in a lab, a bed in a control room.
- **Architecture as class/power:** the same species living differently by stratum (Silksong shows social strata through where insects live and how they look). Rich areas are high and clean; poor areas low and patched.
- **Character by behavior:** bosses and NPCs revealed by what they do. A boss's attack pattern can show desperation or obsession without a word.
- **Silhouette staging:** in high-contrast art (Limbo), silhouettes of other people's traps, fleeing figures, and a giant creature carry the plot.
- **The verb as story beat:** Inside's marching sequence makes the story beat an input: you match the rhythm of the walk or get caught. The player *performs* the world's rule.
- **Murals, statues, ruins:** history in images. Keep them simple enough to read at game scale.
- **Recurring motif:** a symbol, shape or sound that returns in changed contexts (Gris's statues and temple).

## 4. Crowds and systems

To show how a world works without explaining it, show many people obeying (or breaking) a rule. Inside teaches its world by letting you watch crowds: shuffling lines of controlled workers, observers herding them. Then make the player obey or break the same rule. The rule becomes felt knowledge.

Recipe:
1. Show the rule in the background (crowd behaves a certain way).
2. Show the consequence of breaking it (someone gets caught).
3. Make the player perform it (a mechanic based on the rule).
4. Later, let the player break it on purpose.

## 5. Color, light and audio arcs

- **Color as progress:** Gris starts drained of color; colors return one by one tied to stages of healing. Map your palette to the emotional arc chapter by chapter; write it in the story bible.
- **Light temperature:** warm = safety/home, cold = alienation/depth. A single warm light in a cold level is a beacon without the word "beacon".
- **Silence:** Playdead's handling of sound and silence is central to its tension. Remove music before a big moment; the absence is heard.
- **Motif audio:** a character's theme reappears when they're remembered, altered when they change. Celeste's summit merges two themes as the two halves of the character cooperate.
- **Diegetic sounds carry history:** a broken music box, a radio, a factory siren at the same time every loop.

## 6. Lore placement for explorers

- Critical story: on the path, impossible to miss, readable in 2 seconds.
- Supporting story: slightly off the path, visible from the path (the player chooses to go).
- Deep lore: hidden, rewarding the most curious (secret rooms, item text, optional NPCs).
- Every hidden area should reward with **either** gameplay or story, ideally both.
- Item/lore text: ≤ 3 sentences, written from inside the world (a voice, a date, a grudge), never encyclopedia-neutral.

## 7. Clichés to avoid (or subvert)

- Ben Esposito's joke: environmental storytelling is "the art of placing skulls near a toilet". Skeleton + note + blood smear is the default; players tune it out.
- "Dear diary, today the monsters came…" audio logs/notes that exist only to explain.
- Every NPC dying right after delivering information.
- A mural that literally depicts the plot twist.
Subvert by making the environment *contradict* what characters say, or by making the detail funny, mundane, or personal.

## 8. Testing inference

For each room, write down: **"The player should infer: ___."** Then in playtests ask players to describe the room afterwards. If fewer than half mention the thing, strengthen the signal (contrast, placement, camera hold, audio cue). If everyone mentions it in the same words, it may be too heavy-handed.
